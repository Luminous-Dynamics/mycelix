# PRAXIS-NRES-001: Civic Resilience and Democratic Defence Foundations

**Status:** proposed curriculum and policy-research brief for community review. Not an active-service directive, recruitment programme, military doctrine approval, or live training qualification.  
**Course draft:** `examples/courses/civic-resilience-democratic-defence.json`  
**Assessment fixture:** `examples/courses/civic-resilience-policy-benchmark-fixture-001.json`  
**Benchmark specification:** `docs/curriculum/PRAXIS_NRES_002_BENCHMARK.md`  
**Owner boundary:** Praxis owns the learning experience and content lifecycle; Mycelix governance owns course-curation policy; relevant public institutions own official training, service obligations, legal interpretations, and any military doctrine.

## 1. Decision brief: encourage participation, but do not presume universal two-year active military service

The recommended starting position is:

1. **Broadly encourage universal learning** in civic preparedness, emergency response, first aid, cyber hygiene, critical-infrastructure continuity, legal literacy, and democratic oversight.
2. **Offer accessible, paid, useful ways to serve** in military, reserve, civil-protection, emergency medical, engineering, logistics, communications, cyber-defence, infrastructure, and other lawful public-interest roles.
3. **Reserve full-time two-year military pathways for roles and institutions that can demonstrate a specific training/readiness need**, subject to democratic authorization, fair conditions, transparent costs, accessible selection, due process, and applicable conscientious-objection/alternative-service protections.
4. Do **not** treat compulsory two-plus-year active service in the armed forces for all eligible participants as the default or as a conclusion established by current evidence. If a government wishes to consider compulsory service, it must compare military and civilian-service models in public, assess the relevant threat and force requirements, cost the whole system, examine distributional effects, and follow constitutional and legislative processes.

This distinguishes three ideas that can be mistakenly conflated:

- **Learning:** give everyone the opportunity to become more capable of protecting themselves and supporting their community.
- **Participation:** create meaningful and accessible routes to contribute, with recognition and fair compensation.
- **Compulsion:** use law to require a person to surrender two or more years to full-time military service.

The first is broadly supportable; the second should be actively developed. The third requires a much higher evidentiary, legal, institutional, and democratic threshold.

## 2. South African context

This proposal uses South Africa as an initial context because the first community profile may be Johannesburg-based; it is not a claim that one model fits every country.

South Africa's Constitution says the Defence Force's primary object is to defend and protect the Republic, its territorial integrity and its people in accordance with the Constitution and international law. Chapter 11 also requires security services to act in accordance with the law, prohibits members from obeying manifestly illegal orders, requires nonpartisanship, and provides for multiparty parliamentary oversight. The President's authority to employ the Defence Force is constrained by constitutional purposes and parliamentary notification requirements. See the [South African Constitution, Chapter 11](https://www.justice.gov.za/constitution/chp11.html).

South Africa's declaration under the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the Child states that the SANDF is a voluntary force and that there is no compulsory conscription. See the [ICRC treaty-database entry for South Africa](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/crc-opac-2000/state-parties/za). Any proposal that materially changes this arrangement would be a major public-policy and legal question; this learning document neither proposes an unlawful workaround nor substitutes for qualified South African legal advice.

South Africa is not starting from zero on institutional development. In the May 2026 Defence Budget Vote, the Minister said Cabinet had approved a Long-Term Force Evolution Strategy and Defence Planning Concept for a 30-year horizon, with parliamentary engagement to follow. The speech also described the South African National Service Institute (SANSI), launched in 2024, as a youth-service/learnership partnership, reporting that 581 young people had completed training and entered learnerships, with further intakes planned during 2026. See the [2026/27 Defence Budget Vote](https://www.gov.za/news/speeches/minister-angie-motshekga-defence-and-military-veterans-dept-budget-vote-202627-20-may).

**Implication for Praxis:** do not author a competing official SANDF doctrine or duplicate SANSI. Teach doctrine literacy, create transparent evaluation rubrics, compare public options with documented assumptions, and explore curriculum/credential support that complements official and community programmes. Any collaboration, credential recognition, or deployment would require authorization from the relevant institution.

## 3. What international comparisons do—and do not—tell us

Service length is a policy choice shaped by force design, security environment, reserve structure, role complexity, training throughput and legal traditions. There is no universal service length that follows from the fact that national defence matters.

- **Singapore:** the Ministry of Defence states that full-time National Service normally lasts two years. This is a real example of a two-year system, not proof that it is optimal for every country. [Singapore MINDEF FAQ](https://ask.gov.sg/mindef/questions/cmh4fdaqu014juk1bkyuafdnq).
- **Finland:** official Defence Forces material describes differentiated conscript service durations of 165, 255 or 347 days according to the assigned training path, with reserve training thereafter. This illustrates role differentiation rather than a universal two-year active term. [Finnish Defence Forces, Soldier's Guide](https://puolustusvoimat.fi/documents/1948673/2267766/PEVIESTINTAOS_Sotilaan_k%C3%A4sikirja_2020_EN.pdf/2cff30fd-ca71-b12d-6acd-dfde73f21a6e/PEVIESTINTAOS_Sotilaan_k%C3%A4sikirja_2020_EN.pdf).
- **Estonia:** its Defence Ministry announced a move to a standard 12-month conscript period from 2027, with a six-month intensive training period and six months of service practice, linked to its own growing technical-capability and readiness requirements. The Government approved a related legal framework in May 2026. That is a more tailored example of changing service length to meet specific readiness requirements, not a general prescription. [Estonian Ministry of Defence: 2027 service reform](https://www.kaitseministeerium.ee/en/news/starting-2027-duration-conscript-service-will-be-12-months) and [May 2026 legal-framework update](https://kaitseministeerium.ee/uudised/valitsus-kiitis-heaks-lahingvalve-oigusliku-raamistiku).

Comparisons need to include the rest of the system: the number of people actually required, reserve refresher obligations, equipment and instructor capacity, accommodations, pay and benefits, and the trained capability produced. Comparing the headline number of months alone is inadequate.

## 4. Empirical evidence on conscription is mixed

The research does not support a simplistic conclusion that compulsory military service automatically creates better citizens, social cohesion or economic outcomes.

- A 2025 study using Denmark's conscription lottery found limited effects on many long-term civic outcomes, with a positive effect on political interest. It found neither broad civic harm nor the full range of positive civic effects often claimed for conscription. [European Journal of Political Research](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-political-research/article/does-universal-conscription-impose-a-civic-penalty-individuallevel-evidence-from-a-randomized-conscription-lottery/8D01155A1B43A7A1AE006341AFA3E05B).
- A 2025 IZA discussion paper comparing cohorts in 21 European countries reported more negative attitudes toward immigrants among men subject to compulsory service, with effects varying by context. These findings caution against treating conscription as a neutral or automatically inclusive nation-building device. [IZA Discussion Paper 18003](https://www.iza.org/en/publications/dp/18003/serving-countries-shaping-views-military-conscription-and-attitude-towards-immigrants).
- A 2019 Swedish study estimated increased post-service criminal behaviour in particular cohorts, especially among men from lower socioeconomic status households and those with pre-service criminal histories. It is a context-specific historical study, not a general prediction for present-day services. [The Economic Journal](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/129/622/2522/5490322).

These studies answer different questions in different populations. None establishes the operational military effectiveness of a two-year service requirement in South Africa. Any proposal must be evaluated on its intended outcome: trained readiness, reserve availability, civil resilience, skill acquisition, civic capability, or another explicitly defined goal.

## 5. A more useful service architecture

Instead of using one full-time service model for every eligible person, assess a layered, choice-based system. The following is a proposal for research and public deliberation, not a description of existing policy.

### Layer A — universal preparedness education

A short, accessible foundation course for all interested learners—and a candidate for inclusion in schools, vocational training and community education—covering public preparedness, first-aid pathways, official emergency reporting, cyber hygiene, infrastructure dependencies, democratic oversight and lawful public service.

Course completion does not create military, policing, or emergency-response authority. Any professional qualification must still be earned through the relevant accredited institution.

### Layer B — paid public-resilience service options

Provide multiple ways for volunteers or legally eligible participants to contribute, aligned with their abilities and local needs. Examples include civil protection, emergency medical support, cyber defence, water and energy maintenance, communications, engineering, transport, logistics, food and shelter support, and continuity planning. In South Africa, investigate how a learning pathway could complement existing SANSI youth-service/learnership activity, TVET providers, accredited first-aid training, municipal disaster-management structures, and established volunteer organisations rather than invent another delivery institution.

A credible programme needs fair compensation, safety, supervision, insurance, accessibility, portable credentials, independent complaints processes, and a clear distinction between volunteer/public-service roles and military command authority.

### Layer C — professional and reserve military pathways

Use role-specific entry standards, training periods and continuing education. Technical specialists may require longer preparation than general roles; reserve readiness may be maintained through periodic refresher training rather than identical continuous full-time service for everyone.

A two-year track may be justified for a particular professional or specialist role if the institution demonstrates that the added time produces necessary capability and is worth its full cost. It should not be assumed necessary solely because two years is administratively convenient.

### Layer D — civilian alternatives and safeguards

A legitimate national-service design must address medical fitness, disability accommodations, family and caregiving responsibilities, education, loss of earnings, discrimination, conscientious objection, appeal rights, and alternative civilian service. The relevant rules must be established democratically and legally, not invented by Praxis or Mycelix.

## 6. Better defence doctrine and institutional design

Praxis can teach doctrine literacy and support independent, public-interest analysis at the institutional and strategic level. South Africa already has a government-led 30-year force-planning process described above; Praxis should help learners understand, evaluate and discuss such public work, not present its own draft as official doctrine. It should not become a channel for unaccountable operational commands, weapons instruction, combat tactics, or targeting.

A better doctrine-learning framework should contain these components:

1. **Mandate and political authority:** clearly define constitutional purpose, permitted roles, command accountability, parliamentary oversight, civilian-military boundaries, and procedures for challenging unlawful orders.
2. **Capability-based force design:** define necessary tasks and the competencies, personnel, equipment, infrastructure, logistics and training required to perform them. Measure trained capability and readiness, not merely personnel headcount or years served.
3. **Specialist competence:** make medicine, engineering, logistics, communications, cyber defence, infrastructure protection, emergency coordination and sustainment first-class professional pathways.
4. **Readiness as a lifecycle:** define training standards, supervised practice, periodic refreshers, retention, equipment availability, health and welfare, and the time required to restore a capability after it degrades.
5. **Interoperability with civilian institutions:** use explicit responsibilities, contact arrangements, data boundaries and legal authorization when defence institutions support disaster management or essential-service continuity.
6. **Learning organization:** record exercises and after-action reviews, retain dissenting evidence, protect good-faith safety reporting, independently investigate serious failures, version doctrine changes, and verify whether corrective actions actually improve outcomes.
7. **Rights and democratic safeguards:** maintain nonpartisanship, accountability, privacy, independent review, due process, and meaningful remedies for abuse. Neither an AI system nor a reputation score should authorize coercive action.

The core structural shift is from **uniform service duration as a proxy for readiness** to **demonstrated task competence plus sustained, independently measured capability**.

A force may still need compulsory service in a particular threat and legal context, but that conclusion should follow from public analysis of the required capability, not precede it.

## 7. Praxis course prototype

The accompanying proposed course is **Civic Resilience and Democratic Defence Foundations**, a 30-hour draft divided into eight modules:

| Module | Main learning objective |
|---|---|
| 1. Public defence and resilience | Understand the purpose and distinct mandates of defence, civil protection, emergency management and public services |
| 2. Constitution, law and rights | Understand civilian oversight, nonpartisanship, legal limits and accountability |
| 3. Community preparedness | Design an accessible household or community emergency plan |
| 4. First aid and emergency support | Understand safe responder roles and seek accredited practical assessment |
| 5. Cyber hygiene and evidence | Report a simulated incident while respecting provenance and privacy |
| 6. Critical infrastructure | Model essential-service dependencies and feasible recovery |
| 7. Service models and defence institutions | Compare service lengths, role structures, readiness requirements and full costs |
| 8. Capstone | Evaluate a fictional service disruption and produce an evidence-based resilience plan |

The teaching style should be open, critical and nonpartisan. Learners should assess competing proposals—including universal two-year service—rather than be graded on whether they favour conscription, enlistment, or a particular political position.

The draft course defaults to local-only assessment and disables federated learning. It must not create individual enlistment-propensity scores, political-affiliation scores, or citizen trustworthiness profiles. Any future sharing of learner data would require a purpose-limited, consent-aware privacy design.

## 8. How Praxis should measure whether the course works

A companion benchmark specification and a machine-readable fictional service-disruption/policy-comparison fixture have been added in [PRAXIS-NRES-002](PRAXIS_NRES_002_BENCHMARK.md) and `examples/courses/civic-resilience-policy-benchmark-fixture-001.json`. They define four neutral policy alternatives, eight scoring dimensions, eight scenario perturbations, hard-fail rules and a pre-registered learning-evaluation protocol. The fixture is an unactivated draft: no active curriculum graph change or learning outcome has been qualified.

The course should be evaluated against learning and preparedness outcomes, not ideological conformity or recruitment rate.

- **Learning:** pre-course and post-course knowledge tests, with separately scored factual understanding and legal/ethical reasoning.
- **Retention:** repeat assessment after 30 and 90 days, with results reported by module and cohort.
- **Practical skill:** assess first-aid competence through appropriately qualified external instructors; do not treat reading a lesson as certification.
- **Scenario performance:** use synthetic service-disruption exercises to measure correct evidence handling, resource feasibility, service continuity, uncertainty recognition, and appropriate escalation.
- **Transfer:** test held-out scenarios and unfamiliar service-dependency graphs, not only examples shown during instruction.
- **Equity and access:** report enrolment, completion, accommodation, accessibility, cost, and outcomes for diverse learner groups without inferring sensitive traits or labelling individuals.
- **Safety and agency:** audit misleading confidence, privacy leakage, rights misunderstandings, perceived coercion, complaints and course corrections.
- **Cost-effectiveness:** compare the measured improvement and full delivery cost against existing public-preparedness education and accredited training alternatives.

Publish the curriculum, sources, rubric, intended outcomes, limitations and correction history. Independent educators, emergency professionals, civil-liberties experts, public institutions, and learners should be able to challenge both the content and the scoring method.

## 9. Doctrine and service-policy research benchmark

Before proposing a nationwide two-year requirement, create a transparent scenario and cost model comparing at least:

- voluntary professional military service plus reserves;
- a role-specific, shorter conscript model;
- a two-year active-service model;
- universal preparedness education with optional paid service pathways;
- a mixed national-service model that includes civilian and defence options.

For each model, account for the number of qualified personnel produced, readiness by role, instructor and equipment constraints, retention, reserve availability, total fiscal cost, foregone study/work time, health and welfare, equity, accommodations and alternative-service demand. Report uncertainty and make the source assumptions public.

A policy cannot be called superior just because it enrols more people or keeps them in uniform longer. It must show that it produces a relevant capability or public benefit better than competing options, at a cost and rights impact the public has explicitly considered.
