# Evidence-led public comment plan: South African building-law reform (2026)

**Status:** research and consultation-preparation framework, not a submitted comment, legal opinion, or assertion that any reform is scientifically conclusive.

## 1. What is currently supported by source evidence

- The official Cabinet communication described the Bill's broad objectives as promoting uniformity in law across municipalities and safe construction through building science, workmanship and quality materials. It also cited inconsistent application and interpretation of the existing Act as a reason for change.
- The official notice confirms the current consultation extension and deadline. It does not establish the merits of any substantive amendment.
- A 30 July 2026 article by Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr (secondary legal commentary) describes a proposed revision to section 7 intended to use more objectively measurable approval criteria and to remove the existing section's references to disfigurement, unsightliness/objectionability and derogation of neighbouring property values. The article also flags uncertainty about the interaction with section 10, which it says retains similar language.
- The legal commentary also describes proposed powers concerning sustainability, material choice, resource efficiency, environmental quality, professional accountability, building complexity and universal access.

**Caution:** These points are a starting map from the cited sources. Before filing, read the complete primary Bill, compare each proposed clause with the current Act, and obtain South African legal review of any interpretation or claimed inconsistency. Do not present secondary commentary as statutory text.

## 2. A balanced position worth testing with evidence

A defensible objective is to protect life, health, sanitation, structural and fire performance, accessibility, and legitimate environmental/heritage interests while reducing unpredictable refusal based solely on subjective taste or generalized neighbouring property-value objections.

Design freedom should not mean removing demonstrable protections. Nor should public safety become a blanket justification for arbitrary aesthetic control. The policy question is which interests should be protected by objectively framed requirements, where context-specific design controls are legitimate, how they are authorized, and how a property owner can understand and challenge a decision.

The submission should avoid claiming that a proposed change is conclusively better without evidence. State which points are normative principles, which are factual observations, which are hypotheses for impact assessment, and which remain uncertain.

## 3. Provisional recommendations for consultation

These recommendations are hypotheses to assess against the full Bill, existing law, and evidence—not claims that every proposed amendment is currently defective.

### A. Publish objectively testable approval and refusal criteria

Where a local authority refuses a plan, the decision should identify each applicable statutory/regulatory provision, the relevant fact or plan element, the evidence relied on, and the available reconsideration/appeal path. Avoid a free-floating judgment that a lawful design is merely "unattractive" without published, lawfully adopted context-specific criteria.

### B. Reconcile overlapping powers

The cited legal analysis raises a question about the relationship between proposed section 7 changes and section 10's post-approval powers. Compare the primary text and ask the drafters to explain whether the same subjective grounds are intentionally preserved, narrowed, or removed in each stage. If amenity considerations are legitimately retained, ask for clear scope, objective criteria, reasoned evidence, procedural safeguards and review, rather than contradictory decision tests.

### C. Preserve legitimate contextual protections

Heritage areas, protected landscapes, public safety corridors and genuinely evidenced amenity impacts may warrant context-specific rules. Those rules should be made through the legally authorized process, use published criteria and clear geographic scope, disclose the public purpose they serve, involve meaningful consultation, and provide a way to contest inconsistent application. One person's taste should not silently become a universal standard.

### D. Make safe alternative solutions legible

Where existing law permits alternative/performance-based solutions, publish acceptable evidence, reference methods, required professional sign-offs, and decision timeframes. A proposal should be evaluated against explicit outcomes; computer-generated imagery alone is not evidence of structural, fire, health, energy or accessibility performance.

### E. Make national and municipal rules composable without legal ambiguity

Provide an authoritative machine-readable register of clause ID, source edition, effective date, territorial scope, amendments, adoption status, delegated authority and supersession history. National baseline requirements and lawful local additions should be distinguishable. Any priority rule should be grounded in law, not inferred by software from geographic specificity, date, or which standard seems more stringent. Publish change logs and historic editions so a decision can be reproduced.

### F. Evaluate affordability and unintended consequences

Assess how proposal options affect low-cost housing, incremental/self-build processes, rural and urban communities, construction time, professional costs, materials access, accessibility and safety outcomes. Include no-change and non-regulatory options. Do not assume either that stricter rules always improve safety or that deregulation always improves affordability.

### G. Create a post-implementation evidence loop

Require a monitoring plan with baseline data, outcome measures and a review date. Where feasible, publish anonymized aggregate data on approval duration, refusal reasons, appeals and reversals, common missing-document requests, costs, accessibility outcomes and construction-related incidents. Protect personal data. Revisit provisions where observed results contradict the original rationale.

## 4. Evidence matrix to complete before filing strong claims

| Question | Evidence needed | Safe statement before evidence is collected |
|---|---|---|
| Are approval decisions inconsistent across municipalities? | Comparable anonymized decisions, legal text, reasons, appeals and time periods | Cabinet communications identify inconsistent application as a motivation; the scale and distribution need measurement. |
| Do subjective aesthetic grounds create arbitrary outcomes? | Full statutory comparison, decisions/case law, coded refusal reasons, affected-party submissions | The legal commentary identifies an issue to investigate; do not generalize frequency or impact without data. |
| Would objective criteria improve predictability? | Matched cases, inter-rater agreement, review of reasons, processing-time data | This is a plausible hypothesis to test, not yet a measured effect. |
| Would a proposed change reduce costs or increase housing supply? | End-to-end project costs, time, finance and demand data; confounder analysis | Neither effect can be asserted without a defined counterfactual. |
| Could narrowing amenity powers harm neighbours or heritage? | Protected-area evidence, noise/daylight/privacy impacts, heritage consultation and appeal cases | Such impacts are possible and should be assessed rather than assumed absent. |
| Can digital rule composition improve access and consistency? | Pilot corpus, missed/conflicting rules, accessibility/usability tests, update latency | A testable implementation proposal, not a proven policy outcome. |

A useful evidence rule: a simulation is not independent empirical validation; a few anecdotes are not a population estimate; lack of detected harm is not proof of no harm; contradictory results must remain visible. Explain sampling, missing data, uncertainty, confounders and limitations.

## 5. Suggested submission outline

1. Identify the Bill and exact clauses being discussed.
2. State the public interest and the problem the clause is intended to solve.
3. Separate support, requested changes and unresolved questions.
4. For each proposed edit, quote or identify the primary text, explain the intended effect, provide supporting and conflicting evidence, and state the uncertainties.
5. Compare at least a no-change option, the draft proposal, and a narrower safeguard/alternative.
6. Discuss costs/benefits and impacts on residents, homeowners, neighbours, builders, local authorities, accessibility, heritage and housing affordability.
7. Suggest implementable wording only after legal review.
8. Request a published response explaining how material evidence and opposing submissions were addressed.
