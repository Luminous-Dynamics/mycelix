## Draft submission body

**To:** Director-General, Department of Trade, Industry and Competition  
**Attention:** Ms Anna-Marie Lötter  
**Subject:** Public comment on the National Building Regulations and Building Standards Amendment Bill, 2026

### 1. Summary of position

I support modernising building regulation to make decisions more consistent, technically grounded, transparent, and proportionate to the actual risks and outcomes at stake. Building rules should robustly protect life, health, sanitation, structural and fire performance, accessibility, and other legitimate public interests.

At the same time, owners should retain meaningful freedom to create homes that suit their needs, cultures, aesthetics, family structures, accessibility requirements, privacy, and ways of living. A lawful design should not be refused merely because it is unfamiliar or departs from a conventional style. Where the law does protect neighbourhood amenity, heritage, privacy, daylight, noise, or other contextual interests, the criteria and process should be clear enough that applicants and neighbours can understand what is protected, what evidence matters, and how a decision can be challenged.

I recommend that the Bill and any subsequent regulations make the following principles explicit:

1. **Reasoned decisions:** each refusal or condition should identify the legal provision, relevant facts, evidence, applicable criterion, and review/appeal route.
2. **Objective and proportionate tests:** rely on measurable criteria where practicable and explain the lawful role of contextual judgement where measurement is not sufficient.
3. **Coherent powers across the building lifecycle:** reconcile plan approval with post-approval powers so that a ground removed or narrowed at one stage is not reintroduced unpredictably at another.
4. **Fair alternative solutions:** make any lawfully available performance-based or alternative-design path transparent, evidence-based, time-bounded, and reviewable.
5. **Affordable and inclusive outcomes:** assess the effects on housing access, incremental building, small projects, accessibility, construction cost, environmental performance, and the capacity of municipalities.
6. **Transparent rule evolution:** preserve versions, publish changes and reasons, and review actual outcomes after implementation.

These are recommendations about governance and legal clarity, not a claim that any single deregulatory or more restrictive position is proven optimal across all circumstances.

### 2. Section 7 and section 10: please clarify the interaction between approval and post-approval powers

The legal commentary linked above describes the proposed section 7 amendment as replacing existing plan-refusal grounds involving whether a building would disfigure an area, be unsightly or objectionable, or derogate from neighbouring property values, with a test tied to contravention of the Act or regulations. The same commentary notes that section 10 appears to retain overlapping post-approval powers concerning unsightliness/objectionability, nuisance, and neighbouring property values.

I request that the Department and Parliament expressly address this interaction in the explanatory memorandum and final drafting.

- Is the intent to remove subjective appearance/value grounds from plan approval altogether, narrow them, or move their operation to a different stage?
- If similar language remains in section 10, under what specific circumstances may a local authority use it after approval?
- What prevents a proposal approved under the revised section 7 criteria from later being prohibited under materially similar and less clearly bounded criteria?
- What procedures and evidential requirements protect both the owner who has relied on approval and neighbours with legitimate, evidenced interests?

**Provisional recommendation:** establish a coherent set of decision criteria across approval, construction, and post-approval enforcement. If amenity grounds remain, specify their lawful purposes, scope, decision factors, evidence requirements, procedural protections, reasons, and review route. Avoid using an unfettered aesthetic preference or a general assertion of reduced property value as a substitute for an identified, lawfully adopted criterion.

The precise amendment wording should be developed after full primary-text comparison and legal review. The goal should not be to remove legitimate protections for neighbours, but to prevent uncertainty and inconsistency from being used against either applicants or affected residents.

### 3. Protect legitimate local interests without making one aesthetic universal

National consistency should not require every neighbourhood or house to look the same. Nor should personal design freedom be treated as absolute where a lawful, proportionate rule protects a demonstrated public interest.

I recommend distinguishing at least the following categories in regulation and decision practice:

- **Core safety/health requirements:** structural stability, fire and egress, sanitation, safe utilities, and other applicable health requirements.
- **Accessibility and usability:** clearly defined outcomes and accepted methods, including legitimate adaptation for users with disabilities and changing household needs.
- **Environmental and site constraints:** verified flood, fire, geotechnical, ecological, infrastructure, or similar facts, with sources, dates, and uncertainty made explicit.
- **Local context:** legitimate heritage, public-realm, privacy, noise, daylight, or amenity protections where authorised, mapped or otherwise scoped, and based on published criteria.
- **Owner preferences:** architectural style, cultural expression, spatial character, material preference, room relationships, smart-home philosophy, and other subjective design goals. Preferences should not be misreported as legal violations.

Where contextual judgement is necessary, use publicly adopted local plans/criteria, documented reasons, affected-party participation, proportionality, and a review route. Avoid a broad “not to taste” standard that makes outcomes unpredictable. At the same time, do not assume all neighbourhood impacts are imaginary or that every aesthetic criterion is illegitimate; assess the actual legal purpose and evidence.

### 4. Make lawful performance-based and alternative-design paths transparent

Where the Act and adopted regulations permit performance-based or alternative solutions, applicants should be able to determine in advance:

- the performance objective they must meet;
- accepted calculation methods, reference cases, test reports, professional qualifications, and evidence formats;
- the scope and limits of any method;
- who may approve the alternative and what reasons are required;
- how questions, additional-information requests, and decisions are timed and documented; and
- how an applicant may challenge an inconsistent application of the rules.

Digital models, BIM/IFC, simulation, or virtual reality can support understanding and organize evidence, but they do not automatically prove safety, compliance, or satisfactory real-world performance. The evidential status of a model or result should be clearly labelled, and calculations should be validated for their intended use.

I recommend a public consultation process for any detailed performance-based path. Professional judgement and municipal authority should remain intact, but should operate within visible and reproducible rules.

### 5. Establish a machine-readable, versioned regulatory register

The Bill's described proposal for improved national coordination is an opportunity to reduce duplication and inconsistent interpretation. I recommend that the responsible national body publish an authoritative register for building-related instruments, including:

- exact law/standard/regulation identifier and clause reference;
- issuing authority and legal force;
- jurisdiction and project-category scope;
- publication, adoption, effective, amendment, and repeal dates;
- applicable transitional rules;
- links to authoritative source documents and preserved historical editions;
- related national, provincial, municipal, land-use, fire, heritage, energy, accessibility, and environmental instruments;
- known conflicts, interpretation notices, accepted methods, and review decisions; and
- the date each source's currentness was last verified.

The register should distinguish binding law from proposed amendments, future-effective instruments, guidance, engineering references, and owner preferences. A proposed or international reference should not become binding merely because a software tool imports it. Where legal precedence is unclear, the system should show a conflict and direct it for authoritative clarification rather than inventing a winner.

Provide open, machine-readable access, a public change log, stable identifiers, and human-readable explanations. This improves consistency while preserving lawful differences among jurisdictions and the local context where it is genuinely relevant.

### 6. Require impact assessment, meaningful consultation, and post-implementation evaluation

Before major provisions are adopted, assess no-change and alternative options, including narrower safeguards and non-regulatory approaches. Analyse expected benefits and burdens for owners, neighbours, renters, builders, small firms, local authorities, people with disabilities, heritage communities, and low-income households.

I recommend that an implementation and review plan publish, with suitable privacy protections, aggregate measures such as:

- application and approval times;
- reasons for refusal and requests for more information;
- outcomes of internal appeals and court/tribunal reviews;
- the consistency of similar decisions across municipalities;
- construction costs and professional costs by project category;
- safety incidents and verified defects;
- accessibility outcomes;
- energy, water, waste, and other environmental outcomes where relevant; and
- administrative workload and the capacity required to apply the rules.

Set a scheduled review date and revise provisions when outcomes contradict the original rationale. A lack of measured harm is not proof of no harm, and a policy that improves predictability can still create costs that deserve investigation.

### 7. Evidence standard for future changes

For future regulatory amendments, I recommend a public evidence dossier that records the problem, intended outcome, options considered, supporting evidence, counter-evidence, methods, limitations, uncertainties, affected groups, conflicts of interest, consultation comments, and reasons for accepting or rejecting material arguments.

Evidence sufficiency should be judged in the context of the actual risk and discipline. It is not appropriate to apply one universal numerical “evidence score” to fire safety, structural performance, housing cost, heritage, environmental quality, and accessibility. Simulation, empirical studies, incident data, professional analysis, and lived experience can contribute differently depending on the question; their scopes and limits should be made explicit.

### 8. Requests

I respectfully request that the Department:

1. clarify the relationship between the proposed changes to section 7 and the remaining powers in section 10;
2. provide a clause-by-clause explanation of the intended grounds, procedural safeguards, and review mechanisms;
3. explain what evidence and accepted alternative-solution paths applicants may use under the revised framework;
4. publish a plan for a versioned, machine-readable and jurisdiction-aware regulatory register;
5. publish an impact-assessment and post-implementation monitoring plan; and
6. make available a public response explaining how material supporting and opposing submissions were considered.

These recommendations aim to improve safety, legal certainty, consistency, design freedom, and affordability together rather than presuming they are mutually exclusive.
