# Living-City Design Brief: Health Resilience, Civic Beauty, and Mobility (v0.1)

**Status:** research brief / design hypotheses, not an approved plan  
**Scope:** global concept atlas; Johannesburg / Highveld as an initial context for locally validated work  
**Owner boundary:** Mycelix records provenance, rights, review, decisions, and outcomes; Sol-Atlas maps and compares spatial evidence; Symthaea may help generate bounded hypotheses; Symtropy may simulate only where models and inputs are validated.

## Thesis

A city should not need to become hostile, immobile, or socially isolating to respond to a pandemic. The better target is a city whose everyday design already supports health, access, beauty, and mutual aid—and whose buildings, streets, services, and governance can adapt when risks change.

Compactness is not the same as overcrowding. Public transport is not the same as unsafe crowding. Green space is not a substitute for housing, sanitation, clean air, primary care, or reliable public services. No single urban form prevents every pathogen; different threats require different interventions.

## 1. Pandemic-ready urbanism

Design for a portfolio of capabilities rather than a single “pandemic-proof” city.

### Buildings and indoor air
- Make ventilation performance, outdoor-air provision, filtration where appropriate, and maintenance inspectable in schools, clinics, care facilities, transit vehicles, public offices, and other high-occupancy spaces.
- Provide adaptable rooms and operational plans for temporary triage, isolation, remote service delivery, and safe staff rest without permanently turning civic buildings into clinical spaces.
- Treat indoor air as infrastructure: commissioning, maintenance records, responsible ownership, and access to understandable performance information.
- Do not claim a building is safe solely because it has windows, a high energy rating, or a nominal ventilation system. Performance depends on occupancy, operation, filtration, climate, and the specific hazard.

### Public space and neighborhood access
- Provide shaded, accessible outdoor spaces with seating, drinking water, toilets, lighting, and clear maintenance responsibility.
- Ensure parks, walking routes, local markets, and everyday services are distributed equitably; measure access by safe travel time and ability, not only straight-line distance.
- Create flexible plazas and curb space that can support queues, food distribution, vaccination, mobile clinics, deliveries, or outdoor commerce when needed.
- Avoid making public space a substitute for safe housing or indoor air improvements.

### Essential services and continuity
- Map dependencies for water, sanitation, electricity, communications, food, medicines, waste collection, clinics, and public transport.
- Design redundant supply and offline/manual operating procedures for outages and surges.
- Plan paid sick leave, income support, accessible communication, and care support as parts of preparedness; people cannot comply with public-health guidance if doing so means losing food or housing.
- Protect privacy: use aggregate, purpose-limited data for public planning; do not build permanent movement surveillance as a default emergency tool.

### Governance and exercises
- Maintain a publicly understandable emergency playbook with named responsibilities, trigger conditions, procurement options, mutual-aid arrangements, and accessible multilingual communication.
- Exercise scenarios with residents, frontline workers, disabled people, informal traders, older adults, and essential workers before emergencies.
- Record dissent, uncertainty, and lessons learned. Emergency powers must have scope limits, review, and sunset conditions.

## 2. Making cities more inspiring

Beauty is not a decorative layer added after infrastructure. It emerges from comfort, human scale, local authorship, material craft, ecology, cultural meaning, and the ability to participate in shaping shared places.

### Everyday civic beauty
- Design streets as sequences of useful places: a shaded threshold, a safe crossing, a small square, a market edge, a play space, a workshop, a garden, a transit stop.
- Pair shade trees and biodiverse planting with water budgets, soil and contamination checks, drought planning, maintenance funding, and locally appropriate species.
- Support public art, music, maker spaces, libraries, sports, food markets, and cultural events through paid local participation and clear rights agreements.
- Use durable, repairable materials and local craft where appropriate; avoid design gestures that look impressive in renderings but are hostile to daily use or expensive to maintain.
- Make night-time streets welcoming through human presence, lighting, safe crossings, active frontages, and reliable transport—not merely brighter floodlighting.

### Cultural authorship and anti-displacement
- Treat global precedents as questions to investigate, not templates to copy or labels for a population.
- Pay local designers, artists, historians, caretakers, and community reviewers; document attribution and permissions.
- Measure whether improvements raise rents, displace residents or informal livelihoods, privatize commons, or make a place less usable to its current community.
- Give residents meaningful power to amend, contest, restrict, or decline proposed cultural representations and uses of contributed knowledge.

### The “wonder per journey” hypothesis
Every ordinary trip should have opportunities for delight: a memorable view, a tree canopy, a public artwork, a comfortable stop, a lively market, a quiet refuge, or a safe place to meet. Test this through resident-led audits and surveys rather than assuming designers can define inspiration on behalf of everyone.

## 3. Transport that moves people well

The goal is not to maximize vehicle speed. It is to maximize safe, affordable, reliable access to jobs, education, care, food, culture, and one another—with lower emissions and less time lost.

### Network hierarchy
1. **Walking and accessibility first:** continuous sidewalks, curb ramps, safe crossings, shade, lighting, benches, legible wayfinding, and step-free paths to stops.
2. **Frequent, reliable public transport:** dedicated bus lanes where justified, signal priority, safe stations, clear route information, coordinated transfers, and dependable operating hours.
3. **Safe cycling and micromobility:** protected networks where feasible, secure parking, repair access, and connections to transit; never assume all riders are able-bodied or can afford a bicycle.
4. **Interchange and integration:** simple transfers among rail, BRT, buses, minibus taxis, walking, cycling, and paratransit; protect the role and livelihoods of existing operators through co-design and fair transition plans.
5. **Goods and care mobility:** delivery windows, loading areas, emergency access, and routes for people whose trips are not commuting.
6. **Demand and land use:** place more housing, jobs, schools, clinics, food, and public services near reliable transport, while preventing transit-led displacement and protecting affordable tenure.

### Pandemic-adaptive transport
- Design stations and vehicles for good ventilation, clear passenger information, maintainable surfaces, accessible hand hygiene, and operational flexibility.
- Use real-time crowding information only when it is reliable, privacy-preserving, and paired with service alternatives; avoid making access depend on a smartphone.
- Prepare scalable service plans: short-turns, additional peak capacity, temporary bus priority, outdoor waiting space, and targeted support for essential workers.
- Keep public transport funded and operating during shocks where possible. Pushing everyone into private cars can worsen congestion, inequality, emissions, and access for people who cannot drive.
- Set health interventions according to the pathogen, evidence, and public-health authority guidance; avoid permanent infrastructure or policies based on assumptions that every outbreak behaves like COVID-19.

### Reliability before spectacle
A beautiful station is not a successful transit system if buses are infrequent, inaccessible, unsafe to reach, or unaffordable. Prioritize:
- headway and on-time performance;
- complete journey time including access, waiting, transfer, and reliability;
- fare burden relative to income;
- service coverage by time of day and for shift workers;
- crash and harassment risk;
- accessibility for disabled and older passengers;
- passenger-kilometres delivered per operating cost and emissions, interpreted alongside equity;
- operator working conditions and service resilience.

## 4. Johannesburg / Highveld research track

These are hypotheses to verify with local evidence and paid community participation, not claims about every Johannesburg neighborhood.

- **Heat and storms:** combine shade and cool routes with stormwater drainage, flood-safe crossings, tree survival plans, and accessible shelters.
- **Long travel distances and spatial separation:** map access to jobs, clinics, schools, groceries, and cultural spaces by actual multimodal journey time and cost.
- **Multimodal integration:** investigate practical interchanges among Gautrain, Rea Vaya, Metrobus, commuter rail where operating, minibus taxis, walking, and cycling. Verify current service, affordability, safety, and operating constraints before recommending a route or capital project.
- **Informal economy:** co-design safe trading, loading, storage, toilets, water, and shelter instead of treating street commerce as an obstacle to beautification.
- **Service reliability:** include water, sanitation, electricity, waste, and communications in every district scenario; public spaces cannot thrive without maintenance and basic services.
- **Land and legacy risks:** investigate contamination, mine-related ground conditions, floodplains, land tenure, and existing livelihoods before proposing infiltration, greening, construction, or relocation.

## 5. Sol-Atlas / Mycelix data contract

Every proposed intervention should be represented as a testable, versioned hypothesis with:

- geography and spatial scale plus explicit accuracy/provenance status;
- the population and trips or services intended to benefit, without unnecessary personal data;
- baseline and target indicators with units, time window, denominator, source, and known gaps;
- climate, health, safety, accessibility, cost, maintenance, and displacement failure modes;
- source references and rights status;
- local authorship, paid review, consent, dissent, and contestation records;
- scenario/model version, input manifest, uncertainty range, and reproducible run identifiers;
- validation status distinguishing concept, researched, simulated, community_reviewed, pilot_tested, and measured_outcomes;
- accountable owner, maintenance budget, evaluation date, and conditions for stopping or revising a pilot.

Do not combine these into one opaque “city score.” Display trade-offs and distributional outcomes separately. A project that improves average travel time but worsens access for disabled riders or displaces low-income residents should not be described as an unqualified improvement.

## 6. Pilot portfolio and evaluation

Start with reversible, low-risk pilots only after local authority, safety, accessibility, and community review. Candidate categories:

| Pilot | Primary measures | Guardrails |
|---|---|---|
| Shaded accessible route to a high-demand transit stop | shade coverage, heat exposure, footfall, perceived safety, route completion | tree survival, utilities, sightlines, maintenance funding |
| Transit-priority corridor or intersection | headway reliability, end-to-end time, crash risk, passenger throughput | local access, minibus-taxi operator involvement, emergency access |
| Public building indoor-air assessment | measured ventilation/filtration performance, maintenance completion, occupancy assumptions | privacy, procurement transparency, avoid unsupported health claims |
| Flexible neighborhood plaza/market | diverse use, dwell time, access to toilets/water, trader income and participation | no displacement, universal access, noise and upkeep |
| Essential-services continuity map | outage duration, dependency coverage, response time, offline readiness | data minimization, cyber security, no public exposure of critical vulnerabilities |

Establish a baseline first, publish methodology, compare before/after with a plausible control where practical, and report uncertainty. Separate observed outcomes from simulated estimates. A pilot is not a success merely because it was installed or well photographed.

## Success criterion

The atlas should help people make cities healthier, more beautiful, more accessible, more culturally alive, and easier to move through—without hiding uncertainty, exporting harm to less powerful communities, or mistaking attractive visualizations for evidence.
