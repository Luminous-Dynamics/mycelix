# Functional Reconstruction of Amazonian Dark Earth Systems v0.1

**Status:** Research programme and benchmark design only. This is not a formulation, production instruction, soil amendment recommendation, field-trial authorization, or claim that a modern treatment recreates historical Terra Preta.

## 1. The question: what would it mean to recreate Terra Preta?

The phrase *Terra Preta* is often used as if it described one recipe. It does not. Amazonian Dark Earths (ADEs) are anthropogenic soils associated with pre-Columbian settlement and landscape management. Archaeological and soil-science literature distinguishes darker Terra Preta from related brown anthropogenic soils (often called Terra Mulata / Amazonian Brown Earth). Sites differ in formation history, location, soil parent material, cultural materials, land use, and measured properties.

A responsible reconstruction programme therefore asks two separate questions:

1. **Historical reconstruction:** Which coupled human, ecological, mineral, biological, and landscape processes formed a specific documented ADE, and what evidence supports each inference?
2. **Functional analogue design:** Can a locally appropriate, safe, lawful and measurable soil-management system reproduce selected soil functions under specified constraints?

The second is not a claim to reproduce the first. A modern analogue may be useful without being historically or culturally identical. We should say "functionally tested ADE-inspired system" until archaeological and soil evidence justify a more precise description.

## 2. What the evidence suggests — and what it does not establish

### 2.1 A coupled settlement-and-landscape process

The literature associates ADE formation with long-term human occupation and repeated inputs from the surrounding environment: fire-derived charcoal/ash, food and plant residues, animal-derived materials, and other settlement refuse. Reported archaeological matrices can contain pottery, bone, shell, and other cultural material as well as carbon- and nutrient-rich soil. Those materials are evidence of particular human histories, not ingredients to source or copy indiscriminately.

Robinson et al. (2021) describe Terra Preta and related brown ADE as distinct anthrosols within a broader pre-Columbian polyculture-agroforestry landscape, including anthropogenic soil fertilisation, forest enrichment, limited forest clearance for cultivation, and low-severity fire management. This changes the design question from "which additive?" to "which persistent flows and land-management processes maintain which soil functions?"

### 2.2 Char is important, but char alone is not the system

Neina and Glaser (2024) explicitly treat biochar as one component alongside nutrient inputs and soil microorganisms. Their modern-analogue incubation used four Ghanaian and two Zambian soils, rice-husk biochar or charcoal residues, and domestic organic inputs; the materials were incubated aerobically for nine months. The study reported changes in carbon, pH, base saturation, exchangeable cations and plant-available phosphorus. Reported pH responses were highly soil- and material-dependent, with the abstract reporting a final range of pH 7.2–8.2 and increases as large as 2.9 pH units in some cases.

This is evidence that a short modern incubation can shift selected chemical indicators. It is not evidence that a nine-month mixture has become a mature, self-maintaining, millennia-persistent ADE, nor that it improves crop yield, water regulation, biodiversity, or long-term climate balance at field scale. The pH response is a reason to measure and constrain amendment effects, not to pursue alkalinity as a goal.

### 2.3 Long-term carbon stability is a material–soil interaction

A 2025 study of biochar particles from Terra Preta reports oxidized particle surfaces, mineral-organic coatings, and internal C/K/Ca distributions consistent with mineral–organic interactions contributing to persistence. These observations motivate testable hypotheses about surface aging, coatings, and mineral association. They do not yet supply a complete, universally predictive stabilization model.

### 2.4 Co-composting can change nutrient and carbon flows, but effects depend on feedstock

A 2025 field co-composting experiment in northern Ghana compared biochar and uncarbonized feedstocks in poultry-manure/rice-straw mixtures. It found feedstock-dependent carbon-loss behaviour and evidence of ammonium retention by biochar, while microbial biomass and nitrate were similar among treatments. This is a useful process comparator, not proof of universal co-composting benefit or equivalence to ADE formation.

## 3. The paradigm shift: reconstruct functions and flows, not a recipe

We should represent a candidate system as a coupled, time-dependent state rather than a fixed list of ingredients.

### Functional modules to measure

| Module | Candidate state variables / observations | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Pyrogenic carbon architecture | Feedstock identity, process provenance, carbon fractions, aromaticity/oxidation indicators, particle-size distribution, surface chemistry | Carbon stock, surfaces, persistence, and material variability |
| Mineral–organic interfaces | Mineralogy, clay/oxide context, coatings, organo-mineral fractions, CEC and charge behaviour | Nutrient retention and potential long-term carbon protection |
| Nutrient reservoirs and cycling | Total and plant-available P, N forms, K, Ca, Mg, micronutrients, exchangeable bases, leaching and uptake | Fertility depends on availability and timing, not total concentration alone |
| Soil physical structure | Bulk density, aggregation, porosity, infiltration, water-retention curves, hydraulic conductivity, erosion | Root environment, water supply, drainage and resilience |
| Biological succession | Microbial/ fungal community descriptors where justified, microbial biomass, respiration, enzyme/function assays, roots and soil fauna | Transformation of organic inputs and biological feedbacks |
| Plant–landscape system | Crop/variety, roots, cover, rotations, agroforestry, residue return, water regime and local management | Long-term function and input recycling depend on the living system |
| Human and material flows | Lawful biomass sources, residue destinations, nutrient recovery, energy, transport, labour, governance and community consent | Feasibility, circularity, equity, and whole-system environmental balance |

No module should be inferred from the presence of black colour or high total carbon alone. Soil health must be measured as multiple functions with context-specific endpoints.

## 4. A falsifiable research architecture

### Hypothesis family A — formation mechanisms

- A1: Different pyrogenic-carbon materials produce distinguishable changes in surface chemistry and carbon persistence under matched mineral and soil conditions.
- A2: Mineralogy and reactive mineral surfaces explain part of the variation in nutrient retention and carbon stabilization beyond biochar descriptors alone.
- A3: Repeated organic inputs change the nutrient and microbial state in ways that cannot be represented by a one-time carbon addition.
- A4: Plant cover, roots, and residue recycling change the trajectory of soil structure and nutrient cycling relative to an unplanted incubation.
- A5: The interaction among carbon material, nutrient input, mineralogy, biology and management is context-dependent; no universal positive synergy is assumed.

### Hypothesis family B — local functional performance

- B1: A locally matched system can improve a predeclared soil function (for example, plant-available water or nutrient retention) versus the relevant local practice in a specified soil–crop context.
- B2: Benefits and adverse effects vary across soil mineralogy, texture, starting pH, salinity, climate, water regime and crop.
- B3: Any yield or water-productivity improvement persists across more than one growing season and does not depend on a single favourable weather year.
- B4: Nutrient losses, greenhouse-gas fluxes, contaminants, salinity and ecological effects remain within qualified, predeclared limits.
- B5: The net system benefit remains positive after feedstock collection, conversion energy, transport, processing losses, labour, and end-of-life effects are counted.

Null effects, trade-offs, harm, and non-identifiability are publishable outcomes.

## 5. Experimental design: identify interactions without inventing a mega-recipe

A staged design is preferable to a giant unpowered factorial:

1. **Historical evidence synthesis:** classify sites and source evidence; distinguish measured profile observations, archaeological interpretation, modern analogues, and hypotheses. Do not pool different ADE classes as one formulation.
2. **Material and soil characterization:** characterize candidate materials and the baseline soils before any application study. Qualified specialists define contaminant panels, method validity, thresholds and approvals.
3. **Mechanism experiments:** use a small number of preregistered contrasts to isolate material, mineral, organic-input, biological and plant-management contributions. Preserve the experimental unit and independent replicate hierarchy.
4. **Controlled plant/soil experiments:** compare with no-amendment and relevant local-practice comparators; keep nutrient and water programs matched when estimating incremental effects.
5. **Longitudinal field evaluation:** predeclare the crop, site, endpoints, follow-up periods, randomization, blocks, missing-data handling, analysis, and stopping rules. Measure multiple seasons and report adverse outcomes.
6. **Prospective transfer:** lock models and selection policies before evaluation at unseen sites, soil classes, material batches and seasons.

Use fractional-factorial, response-surface, or Bayesian experimental-design methods only where the design retains identifiability of the prespecified causal contrasts. Do not select treatment combinations based on a model's predicted yield without material characterization, permission, and qualified human review.

No archaeological soil, artifact, charcoal, microbial community, or other cultural material should be removed or transplanted for a modern recipe. Work on heritage sites requires the relevant archaeological authorities and affected Indigenous/local communities, with meaningful consent, governance, attribution, and benefit-sharing. Publicly available papers do not imply permission to appropriate community knowledge.

## 6. Safety and legal boundary

This document authorizes no production, treatment, distribution or application.

Any work involving organic waste, manure, urine, wastewater-derived nutrients, ash, industrial residues or other potentially hazardous streams requires source characterization, applicable legal classification, validated treatment and pathogen/contaminant controls, occupational safeguards, disposal planning, accredited/competent laboratory review, and relevant permissions. Untreated human excreta and unknown/mixed waste streams are not suitable inputs to an unsupervised research or community recipe.

For South Africa, exact classification and permissions must be obtained for the actual material, process, intended use and claims. No AI model, Mycelix record, simulation, or synthetic fixture can substitute for those decisions.

## 7. Benchmark: improve a defined function without hiding trade-offs

There is no scientifically meaningful universal scalar called "better than Terra Preta". Use a multi-objective benchmark and report a Pareto frontier. A candidate that increases crop yield but causes unacceptable salinity, contaminant exposure, nutrient losses, or net greenhouse-gas emissions does not win.

| Dimension | Required endpoint / comparison | Proposed acceptance framing |
|---|---|---|
| Soil carbon | Organic and pyrogenic carbon stocks using consistent depth and equivalent-soil-mass methods; input/output accounting | Net stock change with uncertainty; distinguish durable and labile pools |
| Nutrient function | Plant-available nutrients, plant uptake, nutrient-use efficiency, runoff/leaching | Improve the specified nutrient function without excess or loss |
| Water function | Curve-level AWC, infiltration, drainage, plant water status, water productivity | Demonstrate a predefined useful change under local soil/crop conditions |
| Soil structure | Aggregate stability, bulk density, porosity, erosion indicators | Improvement relative to local practice; report depth and method |
| Biology | Microbial/soil-fauna indicators and functional measures selected before analysis | Reproducible change linked to function, not diversity-for-diversity's-sake |
| Crop response | Marketable yield, quality, crop failure risk, nutrient status | Multi-season estimate against relevant local practice with cluster-aware uncertainty |
| Climate | Full lifecycle GHG balance including conversion energy, transport, soil CO2/CH4/N2O, avoided residue fate where defensible | Net effect per hectare and per unit of output with uncertainty |
| Safety | Batch identity, required laboratory tests, contaminants, salinity, pH and ecological risks | Zero bypass of non-compensable release gates |
| Circularity | Fraction of traceable local residues safely recovered, nutrient recycling and competing uses | Report mass/energy balance; no assumed waste is impact-free |
| Economics and access | Cost per verified functional improvement, labour, maintenance, smallholder affordability | Compare to local practice and alternatives; report distribution of costs/benefits |
| Persistence | Repeated measurements and aged-material behaviour | Report observed trajectory; do not infer centuries of permanence from short incubations |

### Comparative baselines

At minimum compare against:
- no amendment / no-change;
- locally relevant conventional soil and nutrient management;
- compost/organic-input-only where scientifically appropriate;
- characterized biochar-only where scientifically appropriate;
- a matched biochar-plus-organic-input treatment;
- mineral nutrient replacement or substitution only when the estimand and nutrient balance are explicit;
- established soil hydraulic or crop models only when their input assumptions and target problem match.

Report treatment effects, uncertainty intervals, per-site and per-season results, and non-inferiority/adverse outcomes where relevant. Use source/site/batch grouped splits for predictive evaluations. A short-term chemistry shift is not a yield result or a permanence result.

## 8. The "better than historical" question

A modern system could be better for a specified purpose: for example, lower contaminant risk, a better nutrient balance, less water loss, improved yield stability in a drought-prone soil, lower net lifecycle emissions, lower cost, or easier local maintenance. It is not realistic to claim it is categorically superior across every function and context.

The defensible ambition is a set of **locally optimized, functionally verified soil-regeneration systems** that:
- preserve the useful mechanisms supported by evidence;
- avoid reproducing historical harms or unsafe material flows;
- use locally sustainable inputs and do not compete with food, habitat, or other beneficial uses;
- adapt to soil and crop conditions;
- improve measured outcomes without externalizing costs;
- remain auditable, repairable, and maintainable by the communities who use them.

## 9. Symthaea / Symtropy / Mycelix research loop

- **Mycelix:** preserve immutable source, batch, sample, method, contrast, custody and trial evidence; prove which records depend on one another; never convert an attestation into proof of the underlying measurement.
- **Symtropy:** simulate physical and biogeochemical state trajectories with dimensional units, conservative balances, explicit boundary conditions, parameter uncertainty and independent solver verification.
- **Symthaea:** maintain competing mechanistic hypotheses, identify missing evidence, choose the next informative *approved* experiment, and abstain outside the evidence domain.
- **Independent verifier:** recompute balances, audit causal estimands and split leakage, check uncertainty calibration, and reject safety-gate bypasses.

The optimization target is not "maximum predicted yield". It is **verified improvement across a stated set of soil functions per unit cost and risk**, subject to non-compensable safety, legal, ecological, and community-governance constraints.

## 9A. Data sources for local context

Keep three source types separate:
- The Soils4Africa 2026 field-observation CSV is a survey-context table; its published schema describes plot, location, land use, crop, erosion and management fields. The publisher says quality control is ongoing.
- The Soils4Africa October 2026 release announcement separately describes more than 11,000 MIR-derived soil-property estimates from 21 countries; these model-derived values need explicit lineage and should not be labelled as wet-chemistry measurements.
- The Soils4Africa field-observation release is a continental context source rather than a treatment-response dataset. Its published tableschema includes plot/location, date, country, land-use, crop, erosion, residue/manure/fertilizer and irrigation information. A 5 October 2026 ISRIC announcement separately describes >11,000 MIR-derived soil-property estimates across >6,100 locations in 21 countries. Preserve the estimate/model lineage and do not treat MIR-derived predictions as wet-chemistry observations.

A separate South African regional reference is the Western Highveld paired NIR/MIR and reference-chemistry dataset (Kock & Van Zijl, 2026; Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17510653). The data article describes 790 composite topsoil samples from cultivated grain fields sampled in winter 2020, with pH (KCl), Bray-1 P and exchangeable K/Ca/Mg/Na. A third-party dataset catalogue reports 803 samples, so the exact Zenodo CSV must be audited before selecting an expected row count. This is a local soil/spectroscopy reference, not natural ADE samples or a biochar-treatment dataset.

These sources can stratify the African/South African research domain and help plan local sampling. None provides the outcome labels required to claim biochar efficacy.

## 11. Claim ceiling

A modern analogue may demonstrate selected chemistry, physics or biology over a defined observation period. It does not establish historical identity, centuries-long persistence, broad ecological superiority, local field efficacy, carbon-removal durability, or commercial readiness. Those claims require separate evidence and independent review.
